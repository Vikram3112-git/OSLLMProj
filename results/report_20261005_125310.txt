Q: What is the difference between preemptive and non-preemptive CPU scheduling?
Check: all key concepts present

**Preemptive vs. Non-Preemptive CPU Scheduling** is a fundamental distinction in operating systems, and it determines how the CPU is allocated to processes.

---

### ✅ **1. Definition**

- **Non-Preemptive (Cooperative) Scheduling**  
  Once a process is allocated the CPU, it **cannot be interrupted** until it voluntarily releases the CPU (e.g., by completing its task, blocking on I/O, or calling `sleep()` or `yield()`).

- **Preemptive (Voluntary) Scheduling**  
  The **scheduler can forcibly take the CPU away** from a running process, even if it hasn't completed its task. This is typically done based on **priority**, **time quantum**, or **real-time constraints**.

---

### ✅ **2. Key Differences**

| Feature | **Non-Preemptive** | **Preemptive** |
|--------|---------------------|----------------|
| **CPU Interruption** | Cannot be interrupted | Can be interrupted |
| **Scheduling Decision** | Made when a process voluntarily releases the CPU | Made by the scheduler at any time |
| **Priority Handling** | Lower priority processes may starve | Higher priority processes can preempt lower ones |
| **Responsiveness** | Lower (especially for real-time tasks) | Higher (can respond to urgent tasks) |
| **Implementation Complexity** | Lower | Higher (requires context switching and preemption logic) |
| **Examples** | FCFS, SJF (non-preemptive versions), some older systems | Round Robin, Priority (with preemption), Real-Time Scheduling (e.g., Rate-Monotonic, EDF) |

---

### ✅ **3. Example Scenarios**

- **Non-Preemptive**  
  A process starts running and runs until it blocks (e.g., for I/O). No other process can run until it gives up the CPU.

- **Preemptive**  
  A high-priority process can **interrupt** a lower-priority process that is currently running, even if the lower-priority process hasn't finished its task.
